Orographic Processes and Land–Atmosphere Interaction

How important are orographic processes for accurately simulating regional climate-change signals, and how do land–atmosphere interactions shape their impacts?

Orographic updrafts and land–atmosphere interactions can modify large-scale circulation differently across model resolutions, leading to distinct long-term change signals.

I use dynamical downscaling to resolve orographic updrafts across a range of model resolutions, from about 100 to 3 km. This allows me to examine how the representation of terrain-driven vertical motion changes with resolution and how those differences affect the simulated climate response. A key finding of this work is that the long-term change in snowpack is closely tied to the signal of change in updraft strength, with different model resolutions producing substantially different snowpack responses.

Land–atmosphere interaction is also critical, as surface temperature and moisture fluxes, when transported into the upper levels through updrafts, interact with large-scale systems and can modify them, making the atmosphere more stable or unstable.

02

Dynamical Downscaling

How does dynamical downscaling help us better understand the climate-change signal?

Resolving regional processes that are poorly represented in global climate models.

I use regional climate models and dynamical downscaling to understand how processes that are poorly resolved in global models influence regional climate signals. I am also interested in understanding how regional climate models can add value when employed with appropriate boundary conditions, bias correction, and parameterization schemes.

My work in high-mountain regions began during my Ph.D. with hydroclimate modeling of Himalayan basins and now extends to the Rocky Mountains. These regions provide an ideal setting for studying complex terrain, snow processes, and fine-scale atmospheric variability.

03

Climate Variability and Change

How do climate-change signals emerge from a background of substantial natural variability?

Separating externally forced change from the variability generated within the climate system.

I study how externally forced climate change and internally generated variability shape regional hydroclimate, with a particular emphasis on snow water equivalent, mountain water resources, precipitation, and related water-cycle processes.

A recurring question in this work is how robust climate-change signals emerge from substantial natural variability. I combine climate-model ensembles, observations, reanalysis, and hydrological information to separate these influences and assess their implications for snowpack evolution and regional change.

04

Climate Extremes

How do circulation, land–atmosphere feedbacks, land-cover change, and climate change shape high-impact events?

Understanding the causality of extreme events and their impacts in a counterfactual world.

My research on climate extremes focuses on the physical mechanisms that shape individual high-impact events and their evolution. I am particularly interested in heatwaves and in disentangling the roles of externally forced change and internal climate variability.

I also study how high-intensity floods could have manifested during different time periods, with less or more human-driven land-cover change.
